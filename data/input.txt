The student sat down to build a language model from scratch, and the first thing she learned was that a model does not see words. It sees numbers. A tokenizer turns text into a sequence of integers, and the model learns to predict the next integer given all the integers that came before it. That is the whole game: next-token prediction, repeated billions of times, until the patterns of language settle into the weights.

She started at the top, with the part she could see. To generate text you call a loop. The loop asks the model for a probability over the next token, picks one, appends it, and asks again. If you always pick the most likely token, the text is flat and repetitive. If you add a little randomness, it comes alive. Temperature controls how bold the choice is. Top-k keeps only the most likely handful. Top-p keeps the smallest set of tokens that together hold most of the probability mass. These are small knobs with large effects.

Underneath the loop sits the model, and underneath the model sits attention. Attention is a way of mixing information across positions. Each token forms a query, every token offers a key and a value, and the query is compared against the keys to decide how much of each value to read. The comparison is a dot product, scaled so it does not grow too large, and then a softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to one. A causal mask hides the future, so a token may only attend to itself and to the tokens before it.

One head learns one kind of relationship. Many heads, running in parallel, learn many. The outputs are joined and projected back into the stream. Modern models share keys and values across groups of heads to save memory, and they rotate the queries and keys by an angle that depends on position, so that the attention between two tokens depends only on how far apart they are. This trick is called rotary embedding, and it is quietly elegant.

Around the attention sits the rest of the block. A normalization step keeps the scale of the activations steady. A feed-forward network, wider in the middle than at its ends, gives the model room to think. Residual connections carry the original signal forward, so gradients can flow back through many layers without vanishing. The same block, stacked again and again, is the entire body of the network. Simplicity, repeated, becomes capacity.

Training is its own craft. The loss is cross-entropy: the negative log probability the model assigned to the token that actually came next. You average it over a batch, take the gradient, and step the weights downhill. The optimizer is AdamW, which keeps a running estimate of the gradient and its variance, and decays the weights gently so they do not grow without reason. The learning rate warms up, then follows a cosine down to a small floor. Gradients are clipped so a single strange batch cannot wreck the run.

She trained the small model on a small body of text, and at first it produced noise. Then it produced letters that clustered into shapes like words. Then it produced words, and then short phrases that almost meant something. Watching the loss fall and the samples sharpen was the most convincing lesson of all. The mathematics on the page had become a thing that wrote.

What remained was to make the model useful. A base model only continues text; it does not follow instructions. So you fine-tune it on examples of instructions and good responses, and you adapt only a few low-rank matrices rather than the whole network, which is cheap and surprisingly effective. Then you teach it preferences, nudging it toward answers people prefer and away from ones they do not, until the raw predictor becomes a careful assistant.

She closed the laptop knowing the shape of the whole thing. Tokens in, attention mixing, blocks stacking, a loss pulling the weights into order, a loop turning weights back into language. None of it was magic. All of it was understandable, one layer at a time, from the top down.
